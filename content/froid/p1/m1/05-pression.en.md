## Safety around pressurised equipment (PE)

Pressure is a force divided by an area: **P = F / A**. The legal unit is the pascal (Pa), a force of one newton on one square metre (1 Pa = 1 N/m²). Pressure is also expressed in bar (1 bar = 10⁵ Pa), psi (1 psi = 6,894 Pa), millimetres of mercury (1 mmHg = 133.32 Pa) or atmospheres (1 atm = 101,325 Pa). To convert bar to megapascals (MPa), multiply by 0.1.

## Types of pressurised equipment

- **Gas**: compressors, cylinders, tanks, piping and fittings, reactor autoclaves.
- **Steam**: boilers, sterilisation autoclaves.
- **Liquid**: hydraulic equipment.
- **Vacuum (negative pressure)**: evaporators, desiccators, laboratory glassware (vacuum pump…), frames (deposition, growth, plasma…), experimental set-ups.
- Any equipment containing a fluid that may be over or under pressure, in normal operation or by accident.

## Inspection of pressurised equipment

Pressurised equipment is subject to statutory inspections throughout its life, starting with the commissioning inspection. After that, the frequency depends on the equipment and on the follow-up method chosen by the operator.

| Type of equipment | Requalification |
|---|---|
| Diving cylinders | every 2 years |
| Vessels and piping containing fluorine, boron fluoride, boron trichloride, hydrogen fluoride, chloride or bromide, nitrogen dioxide, carbonyl chloride or hydrogen sulphide | every 3 years |
| Vessels and piping containing a fluid that is toxic or corrosive to the walls | every 6 years |
| Other vessels, piping and steam generators | every 10 years |

## Marking rules

- **"Horse head" mark**: a stamp showing the equipment passed its tests, introduced by the French royal ordinances of 1823. The Mines service stamp shows a horse head facing left; a centre working for that service uses a head facing right.
- **CE mark**: the equipment meets the essential safety requirements of the European Union and can be sold freely throughout the Union.
- **Pi (π) mark**: the transportable pressure equipment meets the conformity assessment requirements (French order of 29 May 2009, known as the "TMD order", and decree of 28 June 2011).

## Pressure-related risks

If a vessel, pipe or cylinder ruptures, a shock wave with a sudden, violent noise can occur. The burst throws fragments at very high speed, can turn a hose into a whip and a gas cylinder into a projectile. If a cylinder falls or is knocked, its cap or valve can also become projectiles. Operating a regulator under pressure is always risky.

## Risks by type of equipment

- **Gas pressure**: flying fragments if it ruptures, gas leaks that can cause poisoning and explosions.
- **Steam pressure**: jets of steam or superheated water from leaks, fragments if the vessel or tubes rupture suddenly.
- **Liquid pressure**: jets of liquid from leaks, hoses "whipping" if they rupture.
- **Negative pressure (vacuum)**: implosion from a knock or spontaneous implosion of a weakened material (cracked glass, for example).

## Risks from gases and cryogenic liquids

Consider the chemical nature of the product and the precautions for cylinders (transport, handling, commissioning).

**By type of gas**

- **Oxidising** (air, oxygen, nitrous oxide, chlorine…): they enable and sustain combustion.
- **Flammable** (hydrogen, carbon monoxide, acetylene…): they burn or explode with an oxidiser.
- **Neutral or inert**: if they leak, they can cause asphyxiation through lack of oxygen (anoxia).
- **Toxic** (sulphur dioxide, arsine, phosphine, hydrogen sulphide…): poisonous above a certain concentration and exposure time.
- **Corrosive** (sulphur dioxide, hydrogen bromide, chloride, fluoride and iodide…): they attack skin, mucous membranes, clothing and metals.

**By handling and physical state**

Cylinders are heavy: moving them can cause back pain and, if they fall, bruises or fractures. Depending on the product's state:

- skin burns and frostbite from very low temperatures;
- burns to the airways from inhaling cold gas (helium at -40 °C);
- explosion from strong liquid-to-gas expansion (dry ice in a fridge, faulty safety valve on a Dewar flask…);
- asphyxiation from falling oxygen levels (1 litre of liquid nitrogen gives 650 litres of gas at room temperature);
- fire from oxygen enrichment around cryogenic containers.

## What to do after an accident

An explosion causes damage through its shock wave (blast effect) and the release of dangerous products. Victims can suffer many injuries (ears, digestive system…). Call the emergency services quickly (first aiders, fire brigade) and know what to do. If the released products are dangerous:

- protect yourself before stepping in (self-contained breathing apparatus or mask, gloves…);
- evacuate the area;
- call the emergency services and, if needed, have the premises decontaminated by a specialist company before using them again.
