## Principle

Welding heats the metal of each part beyond its melting point. As the weld pool at the boundary between the two parts cools, the metal becomes continuous across the weld: the two parts become one. The heat comes from an electric arc maintained between the covered electrode and the workpiece, connected to the poles of the welding set; the arc gives off heat of about 3600 °C and intense light.

The set-up comprises the power source (DC or AC, on a single-phase or three-phase supply), the primary cable, the secondary cables, the electrode holder, the earth clamp (<span lang="fr">prise de masse</span>) and the welder's helmet.

The covered electrode has a core, which is the filler metal, and a coating that helps strike the arc, protects the weld pool and stabilises the arc as it turns to gas. The core must be the same metal as the parts being joined.

## Setting the current

The current depends on the thickness of the parts, the electrode diameter, the welding position and the type of joint. Charts give the best current. For flat welding a rule of thumb is:

I = 50 × (electrode diameter in mm - 1)

This gives 110 A for a 3.2 mm electrode and 75 A for 2.5 mm. Add 20% for an inside corner weld and subtract 20% for an outside corner.

Too low a current makes striking difficult and penetration poor. Too high a current makes striking easy, but the electrode melts too fast, spatter is heavy, the bead collapses and the coating breaks down.

## Polarity

Polarity says which terminal of the set the electrode is connected to, on DC. It depends on the electrode: follow the manufacturer's marking on the pack (AC, electrode positive or electrode negative).

| Polarity | Connection | Usual effect |
|---|---|---|
| Reverse | Electrode positive | Stable arc, good penetration; usual with basic electrodes |
| Straight | Electrode negative | Fast electrode melting, wider and shallower pool, useful on thin sheet |
