Diagnosing a burnt-out motor is a difficult decision. Before measuring, switch off, lock off the installation and check that it is dead.

Most faults come from:

- excessive motor overheating;
- excessive current of electrical origin (prolonged voltage drop, overvoltage, wrongly set safety devices, loose connections, faulty contactor...);
- a refrigeration cause (excessive HP, acids in the circuit...);
- a mechanical cause (seizure from lack of oil...).

## A winding may be open

The ohmmeter across the winding reads a very high resistance, tending to infinity, instead of the normal value. A typical motor winding has at most a few tens of ohms on small motors and a few tenths of an ohm on large ones. The motor (or compressor) must be replaced, or rewound where possible.

## Short circuit between two windings

The wiring must be removed, and the links on three-phase motors, marking everything so it can be reconnected without error. On a high range, the ohmmeter should read infinity between two windings; if it reads 0 Ω or a very low resistance, there is a short circuit. On a single-phase motor, whose common C is wired internally, it is less obvious: the three readings C-A, C-P and A-P give low, inconsistent values (A-P no longer equals C-A + C-P). A short circuit means replacing or rewinding the motor.

## A winding may be earthed

The insulation resistance of a new motor, between each winding and earth, may reach 1000 MΩ. With age it falls, possibly to 10 to 100 MΩ. Replacement should be planned from 1000 kΩ (1 MΩ), and below 500 kΩ the motor is no longer usable.

With a dead earth, the ohmmeter between a terminal and the frame reads 0 Ω instead of infinity (scrape off the paint for a good contact). Measure on each terminal, on the highest range. A partial earth fault only shows with an insulation tester (<span lang="fr">mégohmmètre</span>), which tests insulation at 500 V DC. Never test the insulation of a hermetic compressor while it is under vacuum: insulation is reduced and the winding can be damaged.
