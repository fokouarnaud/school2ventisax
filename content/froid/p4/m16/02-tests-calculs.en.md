Leak testing is usually done before commissioning, before the refrigerant charge. Indirect methods tell whether a circuit is tight or not, but do not locate the leak.

## Calculating the leak rate

The mass lost follows from the pressure change Δp in volume V at temperature T (in kelvin):

m = Δp × V × M / (R × T), with R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) and M = 0.102 kg/mol for R134a.

For 1 m³ at 20 °C (293 K), a 10 mbar (1000 Pa) change in 24 hours gives 1000 × 1 × 0.102 / (8.314 × 293) = 0.042 kg per day, that is **15.3 kg/year** of R134a.

## Pressure decay (circuit under nitrogen)

The system is filled with dry nitrogen through a regulator up to the maximum working pressure, then the pressure change is measured over a time that depends on the volume. Temperature must be recorded, as pressure varies with it. This is the most common method at the end of installation, before charging.

**Example**: 1 m³ system in a room at 20 °C, under 8 bar of nitrogen; pressure drops 10 mbar in 24 hours. The equivalent R134a leak rate is 15.3 kg/year.

## Pressure rise (circuit under vacuum)

The system is evacuated to about 1 mbar absolute with a vacuum pump, and read on a vacuum gauge. The pressure difference with the outside is about 1 bar: a leak lets air in, converted into a refrigerant leak by a correction factor. Temperature must also be recorded.

**Example**: 1 m³ system at 20 °C, evacuated to 1 mbar; the pressure rises by 10 mbar in 24 hours. The equivalent R134a leak rate is about 23 kg/year.

## Gauge check (running circuit)

Same principle as pressure decay, but on a running circuit: a pressure fall may come from refrigerant lost through a leak. The method assumes steady running, which is not true of most systems.

| Case | Situation | R134a leak rate |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 m³ under 25 bar of nitrogen, 0.5 bar drop in 24 h (gauge resolution), 20 °C | 765 kg/year: the safety cut-out would have tripped long before |
| 2 | 20 litres under 13 bar of nitrogen, 0.5 bar drop in 24 h | 15.3 kg/year |
| 3 | 1 m³, 0.3 mbar drop in 24 h | 0.46 kg/year, about 500 g/year: invisible on a gauge |
| 4 | 1 m³ holding 100 kg of R134a, losing 20% of the charge in a year | 13 mbar in 24 h: undetectable on a gauge |

Circuit gauges are not precise enough to show small leaks: a large loss can go unnoticed until the machines cut out.

## Room detectors

They monitor refrigerant in room air, for occupants' safety, and can also reveal a leak. The most sensitive (about 10 ppm) are infrared. They must be placed as close as possible to likely leak sources, following the manufacturer's advice and the regulations (<span lang="fr">réglementation</span>).
